Overview

UT Baja Racing is a new team at the University of Toronto, building the school's first-ever Baja SAE competition vehicle. As Co-Lead of the Brake Team, I'm responsible for the complete braking system subassembly, from deriving performance requirements through detailed design and integration with the rest of the vehicle.

There was no prior vehicle, no design files from previous years, and no institutional knowledge to reference. Every requirement, calculation, and design decision had to be built from scratch.

Reflections

Ali and I started this project without a team, without a baseline vehicle, and, honestly, without driver's licences. We were figuring it out as we went: watching YouTube videos, Googling "how to design a race car braking system," and working through technical manuals from the 1980s to derive equations for a car that had never existed.

The hardest part of co-leading isn't the engineering. It's that there's nobody above you to tell you what to do. Every assumption, every parameter, every decision about what's good enough had to come from us. The team has grown since then, but those early months of just two people compiling a spreadsheet from scratch taught me more about engineering judgment than any course has.

Analysis & Design

Performance requirements were derived analytically in Microsoft Excel, cross-referenced against two independent calculation methods: one based on an IJRET publication, the other on a Northern Arizona University Baja team report, to validate assumptions across the board.

Starting from an estimated vehicle mass of 300 kg at 17.78 m/s, the spreadsheet works through static and dynamic axle loads, rate of retardation, braking torque per wheel, and rotor pad forces. The key system-level outputs: a total required ground braking force of approximately 1,760 N, distributed 60/40 front-to-rear, with a 6:1 pedal ratio producing 1,800 N of master cylinder force from a 300 N pedal input.

Caliper bore diameters were then sized from the required clamping force and true brake line pressure, with front bore at approximately 25.8 mm, rear at approximately 27.0 mm, closing the loop from driver input through to wheel-level braking force.

CAD & Integration

With the analytical foundation complete, the team encountered a budget constraint: building the vehicle from scratch was no longer feasible. The decision was made to acquire a used buggy and retrofit it, which shifted the braking system work from clean-sheet design to adapting calculated specs against existing vehicle geometry.

I'm currently modelling the braking system in SolidWorks, working through component placement and integration with the donor vehicle's frame. Coordination continues with chassis, suspension, and drivetrain leads to ensure system-level compatibility.
